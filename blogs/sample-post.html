Why I Rebuilt My Profiler in Rust

Every performance tool starts the same way: something is slow, nobody knows why, and the existing dashboards raise more questions than they answer. That was the state of our continuous-profiling setup a few months ago — accurate most of the time, but slow enough under load that people quietly stopped trusting it during incidents, which is exactly when you need it most.

The bottleneck was the tool itself

The original sampler was written for convenience, not throughput. It worked fine at low QPS, but the moment we pointed it at a genuinely busy service, its own overhead started showing up in the traces it was supposed to be measuring. Debugging a hot path with a profiler that adds its own heat is a bad time.

So I rewrote the sampling core in Rust, kept the existing collector protocol so nothing downstream had to change, and benchmarked it against the old JVM-based version across the same synthetic workloads. The numbers were the fun part: run-to-run variance dropped from roughly 25% to under 2%, and we caught a genuine 3x latency regression in staging that the old tooling would have buried in noise.

What actually mattered

Rewriting for speed is the easy pitch. The harder, more valuable part was making the tool boring and predictable enough that people would trust its output during an actual incident, at 2 a.m., without double-checking it against three other dashboards first.

Takeaway: a profiler that lies quietly is worse than no profiler at all — measure the overhead of your own tooling as rigorously as you measure the thing it's pointed at.
